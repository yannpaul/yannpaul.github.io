What Is Event-Driven Architecture?

And why LinkedIn built Kafka to get one.

In a request-driven system, a service asks another service for something and waits for the answer. In an event-driven system, a service announces that something happened — an event — and doesn't wait for anyone. Whoever cares can listen later, on their own schedule.

That's the whole idea. Everything in this workspace — the strengths, the weaknesses, the mitigations — falls out of that one shift: from asking to announcing.

The problem that forced the shift

In 2010, LinkedIn had a concrete problem. Every action on the site — a view, a click, a connection request — needed to reach a growing pile of downstream systems: search indexing, recommendation models, monitoring, offline analytics in Hadoop. The tools available at the time were built for one of two jobs: batch pipelines that moved data in nightly dumps, or message queues that pushed data at a consumer as fast as it arrived, with no memory of what had already been sent.

Neither fit. Batch was too slow for anything real-time. Queues had no durable record — once a consumer read a message, it was gone, so a new consumer (or a consumer that crashed and restarted) couldn't catch up. LinkedIn's engineers, led by Jay Kreps, Neha Narkhede, and Jun Rao, built Kafka to close that gap: a system that could take a firehose of "this happened" facts and let any number of independent consumers read them, at their own pace, from any point in the past. LinkedIn open-sourced it in 2011.

Why the name

Kafka was named for Franz Kafka — Kreps has said he thought of it as "a system optimized for writing," a pun that stuck. Whatever the origin story's exact framing, the design goal was concrete: a durable, ordered, replayable log that many independent systems could read from.

The core mechanism: the log, not the queue

This is the detail that makes Kafka worth using as your example for the whole rest of this workspace: Kafka doesn't delete a message once it's read. A topic is an append-only log, split into partitions. Each consumer just tracks its own position — its offset — in that log. Consumers can be added later, replay from the beginning, or fall behind and catch up, all without the producer knowing or caring.

Compare that to a traditional queue (think early RabbitMQ usage patterns): a message is delivered to one consumer and then it's gone. That's fine for task distribution, but it means a new consumer added next month has no way to see last month's events. Kafka's retained, replayable log is what makes it a backbone for an architecture, not just a messaging library.

Why couldn't LinkedIn's existing queueing systems serve as the backbone for many independent downstream consumers?

  • Queues simply could not handle LinkedIn's growing message volume at all.
  • Queues delivered messages to consumers in the wrong order every time.
  • Queues discarded each message right after delivery, leaving late consumers stuck.
  • Queues processed data too slowly for any downstream system to use.

Producers and consumers never meet

The other half of "event-driven": a producer that writes to a topic has zero knowledge of who — if anyone — is consuming it. Add a new consumer tomorrow (a new fraud-detection service, say), and the producer's code doesn't change at all. That decoupling is the seed of Lesson 2's strengths and Lesson 3's weaknesses alike — it's the same design decision cutting both ways.
