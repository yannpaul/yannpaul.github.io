Review and Transfer

Two different tests, on purpose: recall across all seven lessons, then applying it somewhere new.

This lesson has two parts, and they test different things. Part 1 is quick, interleaved recall across every lesson — a fluency check. Part 2 is one new scenario, in a domain this workspace never touched, with no multiple choice — think through each question yourself before revealing how it maps to what you've learned. Fluency without transfer is an illusion of mastery; this lesson is designed so you can tell the difference.

Part 1: Quick Recall

Seven questions, one per lesson, deliberately out of lesson order.

A topic has 6 partitions and a consumer group has 3 members. One member crashes. What happens to its 2 partitions?

  • Kafka permanently deletes those two partitions' stored topic data instead.
  • They stay unread until the crashed member restarts itself again.
  • They're promptly reassigned to the two remaining members via rebalance.
  • The whole consumer group stops consuming until someone intervenes manually.

A service updates its own database, then crashes before publishing the corresponding event. What's the resulting risk, by name?

  • The dual-write problem — consumers silently never learn the change.
  • A rebalance pause, triggered by the crash's partition reassignment process.
  • Event entropy, since far too many services read this topic.
  • The read-your-writes problem, since that local write never fully committed.

Why does a transactional outbox add a small publish delay compared to an unsafe, direct publish?

  • Kafka enforces a mandatory cooldown between every broker's acknowledgment step.
  • The local database transaction itself now commits noticeably more slowly.
  • Consumer-group rebalancing runs automatically after every single outbox table write.
  • A separate relay must read and send that outbox row.

In the log-as-source-of-truth pattern, why does the writer lose instant read-your-own-writes?

  • Kafka blocks every read until all replicas fully acknowledge writes.
  • Its local view is just another consumer, with equal lag.
  • The writer's own transaction gets rolled back after each publish.
  • Schema registry validation delays writes until a human approves them.

What did Uber build specifically to make their Kafka pipeline trustworthy at scale?

  • Chaperone — an audit system counting messages at each stage.
  • A second, fully redundant Kafka cluster running in shadow mode.
  • A custom fork of Kafka replacing its default replication protocol.
  • An internal ban on services publishing more than one topic.

Two services need their writes to succeed or fail together, atomically, right now. Per Helland's framing, what should this be?

  • An activity, coordinated across two services via retried at-least-once messages.
  • A saga, with a compensating action ready for each step.
  • Two entities, kept in sync by a shared Kafka topic.
  • One entity — handled entirely inside a single local transaction.

Why couldn't LinkedIn's original queueing systems serve as a backbone for many independent, potentially-future consumers?

  • Queues delivered every message to consumers in the wrong order.
  • Queues simply couldn't handle the sheer growing message volume involved.
  • Queues discarded each message right after delivery, leaving nothing behind.
  • Queues processed each message far too slowly for real use.

Part 2: Apply It — A New Scenario

A hospital is building a bedside vitals-monitoring platform. Each bed's monitor emits a vitals event (heart rate, SpO2, blood pressure) roughly once a second. Three things need to happen: (1) a nurse-facing dashboard must raise an alert within seconds if a vital crosses a dangerous threshold; (2) a research database and a separate ML team both want to consume the same vitals stream independently, without coordinating with the platform team first; (3) a billing service must produce an accurate, auditable record of which monitoring services were rendered per patient stay, reconciled against the hospital's financial system, with zero tolerance for silently missing an event.

Nothing here is Kafka, LinkedIn, or e-commerce — that's deliberate. Think through each question before opening it. Write down an actual answer, even a rough one; comparing a real attempt against the reveal is what makes this exercise worth doing.

1. Which piece of this system is a transactional aggregate, and which pieces are Helland-style activities? Sketch the boundary first.

The billing ledger for one patient stay is the aggregate: charge line items should be created and reconciled atomically against that stay's account, in a local transaction — not an event, because "this charge is now billed" needs to be atomic with nothing else. Everything downstream of the bedside monitor is an activity: the alert dashboard, the research database, the ML pipeline, and even billing's own ingestion of vitals events, because none of them can be atomic with the monitor's act of measuring a vital sign — monitor, dashboard, and billing are all independently-failable systems. The boundary isn't "billing vs. everything else"; it's "inside one stay's ledger" versus "everyone reacting to a fact that already happened."

2. The alert must not silently miss a critical threshold breach. Which specific mechanisms from Lessons 2–4 would you reach for, and why?

Durability first: replication factor and min.insync.replicas/acks=all (Lesson 2), so a broker failure can't lose the event before anyone reads it. Then delivery: Kafka is at-least-once by default, so the alerting consumer needs to be idempotent (Lesson 4) — a duplicated read shouldn't double-fire an alarm, since a barrage of duplicate alerts trains nurses to ignore them, which is its own failure mode. Finally, observability (Lesson 4's fifth mitigation): alert on consumer lag for the alerting service specifically, because a silently lagging alert consumer is functionally identical to a missed alert — publishing the event guarantees nothing about whether anyone saw it in time.

3. Billing needs to reconcile "service rendered" against actual vitals events weeks later, with zero silent loss. Outbox (Lesson 4) or log-as-truth (Lesson 5) for billing's ledger? Does read-your-writes matter here?

Outbox, not log-as-truth, for the ledger itself. Billing's charge-creation is the transactional core from Question 1 — it needs an immediate, synchronous local write it can act on right away, which is exactly what outbox preserves (the database write stays synchronous; only the publish is relayed asynchronously). Log-as-truth would hand billing the read-your-writes problem for no benefit — billing doesn't need to be "just another consumer" of the charges it just created. Where log-as-truth does fit: the reconciliation job itself, replaying the vitals topic from any point to independently rebuild "what actually happened" and check it against billing's ledger — that's Lesson 2's replay strength, and read-your-writes doesn't apply because a reconciliation job isn't the writer, it's an auditor.

4. The ML team starts consuming the vitals topic without telling the platform team. What strength made that possible, and what should the platform team now worry about?

The strength is Lesson 2's fourth one, directly: producers don't know who's listening, so a new consumer attaches with zero coordination or redeploy. The worry is Lesson 3's event-entropy and schema-evolution risk: the vitals event's shape is now an implicit contract with a team the platform group doesn't know exists. If a field's meaning changes later — say SpO2 moves from a percentage to a fraction — nothing breaks loudly; the ML model just silently degrades on bad input. This is exactly why Lesson 4's schema-registry mitigation matters most precisely where it's least visible: the unplanned consumer you don't know about is the one a breaking change hurts first.

Pick whichever of these four you answered least confidently and tell me your actual reasoning, not just whether you got it "right" — that's the conversation worth having, and it's exactly the kind of moment a learning record gets written from.
